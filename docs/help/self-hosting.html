Running the server side yourself

Lemur records on the PC, but the heavy lifting (speech to text, telling voices apart, naming people, writing the summary) happens on servers you run. This guide lists those servers, the models they run and the hardware they need, then walks through setting them up.

How sure is each number?

measured numbers come from the author's working setup (October 2026). estimate numbers haven't been tried; treat them as a starting point. untested marks things nobody has run yet. The setup commands are examples to adapt: versions and flags change, and only the author's own setup has been measured.

Long recordings: anything over 5 minutes goes through Parakeet in 5-minute pieces, while pyannote works on the whole file, so each person keeps one label from start to finish. Lemur uploads one file per meeting; don't split recordings yourself.

Voiceprint models: Lemur has built-in matching numbers for TitaNet-L, worked out on 47 people from the AMI meeting corpus. It recognises a model by what it answers, not by its name. Any other model (the voiceprint service's compose file defaults to the smaller, faster WeSpeaker ResNet34) needs llmmr voices benchmark first, or voice recognition skips with no matching numbers for this model yet.

End to end measured: a 48-minute Teams call had its notes 6½ minutes after it ended. Mixing took under a second, transcription 3 min 28 s, naming speakers 63 s, the summary 1 min 54 s, and writing the note with its MP3 5 s.

Choosing the summary model

Lemur sends the whole speaker-labelled transcript in one request, so the model needs:

  • An OpenAI-compatible /v1/chat/completions endpoint. Models that think before they answer are fine: Lemur drops <think> blocks.
  • Enough context. A transcript is about 300 tokens per minute of meeting (measured: 53 KB for 43 minutes). Add up to 16k tokens for the answer (summary.maxTokens, which includes the model's thinking) and about 2k for the instructions:
    Longest meetingContext needed
    1 hour36k tokens
    2 hours55k tokens
    Give it 64k as a minimum, 128k to be comfortable.
  • Room to think. Set summary.maxTokens to at least 16384. A reasoning model used up to 13,500 tokens on the introductions check of a 17-minute meeting, and ran out at 8,192 on a 48-minute one.
  • Speed. With the Scriberr backend, Scriberr waits 5 minutes for the summary, then gives up. A model that needs longer on your longest meetings never produces a summary for them. (Sending the summary straight to the model is planned.)

Measured comparison. Five meetings from the AMI corpus that come with human-written summaries, Lemur's own instructions, and a 32k-token limit. An AI judge that didn't know which model wrote what scored each note out of 20 and counted its factual errors.

Tip

In LiteLLM, give the summary model a neutral name such as meeting-summary. Then you can swap the model behind it without touching anyone's PC.

Words it gets wrong

Speech recognition writes names it doesn't know as words it does: a product, a customer or a colleague's name comes out as something that sounds like it. Each person's vocabulary in Lemur lists the right spellings and what they're misheard as. What happens to it depends on the transcription model:

TranscriptionCan it be told the words?What Lemur does
Scriberr with NVIDIA Parakeet or CanaryNo. Scriberr v1.2.0 passes no word list to them. Fixes the words in the transcript after it comes back
Scriberr with a Whisper (WhisperX) profileYes, as its initial_prompt Sends the words, unless the profile has a prompt of its own, and fixes the rest afterwards
An OpenAI-compatible Whisper serverYes, as the request's prompt Sends the words, unless extraFields already has a prompt (an empty one sends none, for a server that refuses the field), and fixes the rest afterwards

Whatever the model, the transcript the summary model reads already has the listed mishearings fixed, and it's told how the vocabulary's words in that transcript are spelled. It isn't told the others: given the whole list, a summary model listed words nobody had said under "Concepts and terms", with meanings it made up.

NVIDIA's NeMo can boost words for Parakeet as it decodes ("GPU-PB" phrase boosting, from a list of key phrases), without retraining. Scriberr doesn't use it yet; a transcription server that does would hear the words right in the first place.

Example builds

  1. The author's setup measured. It works well, but it's three machines that already existed, not a shopping list.
    • Transcription and voiceprints: a Dell Precision T5820 with an RTX 3090 24 GB, Docker on Unraid.
    • Summary: a server with 3× Radeon AI PRO R9700, vLLM (ROCm) serving Qwen3.8-Flash-Next.
    • Gateway: LiteLLM in a small Proxmox container, behind Nginx Proxy Manager with TLS.
  2. One workstation, two graphics cards untested.
    • An NVIDIA 12–16 GB card for Scriberr, plus a 32 GB card (RTX 5090 or Radeon AI PRO R9700) for a 27B-class summary model at 4-bit with 64–128k context.
    • 8 or more CPU cores, 64 GB of memory, a 2 TB SSD, everything in Docker on Linux. The simplest box that should match the measured quality and speed.
  3. Quiet and low power (half measured).
    • Summary: a Framework Desktop or another Ryzen AI Max+ 395 machine with 128 GB, running Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (fast, less careful) or Qwen3.8-27B (careful, 6–8 minutes a meeting).
    • Transcription still needs an NVIDIA card in another machine. Transcribing on its processor is untested.
  4. One 24 GB NVIDIA card for everything untested.
    • Scriberr, plus a ~14B summary model at 4-bit in the rest of the card's memory.
    • The cheapest, but nobody has checked a 14B model's summaries yet. Compare a few notes with your own before relying on it.

Set it up step by step

This puts every service in Docker on Linux, behind HTTPS names on your network: scriberr.example.lan for Scriberr and llm.example.lan for LiteLLM. Replace the names, hosts and keys with your own. Everything below is an example to adapt.

1. Before you start

  • Docker with Compose on each server.
  • For NVIDIA cards: the NVIDIA driver and the NVIDIA Container Toolkit, so containers can use the card. Check that docker run --rm --gpus all <any CUDA base image> nvidia-smi lists it.
  • Two names in your network's DNS, for example scriberr.example.lan and llm.example.lan, pointing at the machine that will run the reverse proxy.
  • A Hugging Face account, to download the models once. Accept the conditions of pyannote/speaker-diarization-community-1 on its model page, and create a read-only access token.
  • Random secrets for the keys below. openssl rand -hex 32 makes one.

2. Scriberr

scriberr/compose.yaml 
services:
  scriberr:
    image: ghcr.io/rishikanthc/scriberr-cuda:v1.2.0   # pinned: Lemur talks to its API
    restart: unless-stopped
    ports:
      - "8080:8080"
    volumes:
      - scriberr-data:/app/data          # uploads, transcripts and its database
      - scriberr-env:/app/whisperx-env   # model environments and downloads (several GB)
    deploy:
      resources:
        reservations:
          devices:
            - driver: nvidia
              count: all
              capabilities: [gpu]

volumes:
  scriberr-data:
  scriberr-env:

Start it with docker compose up -d and open http://<server>:8080. Check Scriberr's own README for its current ports and settings. Then, in Scriberr's web page:

  1. Create your account.
  2. Make a transcription profile: model NVIDIA Parakeet (parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3), diarization pyannote community-1, with your Hugging Face token. Mark it as the default, or put its name in each PC's Profile setting.
  3. In Scriberr's LLM settings, point it at LiteLLM (https://llm.example.lan/v1, after step 5) with a LiteLLM key of its own, and choose meeting-summary as the summary model.
  4. Under Settings → API keys, create a key for each person who'll use Lemur.

The first transcription takes longer: Scriberr downloads Parakeet and pyannote and prepares their environments.

Scriberr keeps the Hugging Face token in plain text

It's stored in every job's settings. Use a read-only token that can do nothing else.

3. Voiceprint service (only for voice recognition)

The service is in Lemur's source code, in server/voiceprint; its README.md covers every option. Copy that folder to a server, then:

cd voiceprint
mkdir -p models
curl -L -o models/nemo_en_titanet_large.onnx \
  https://github.com/k2-fsa/sherpa-onnx/releases/download/speaker-recongition-models/nemo_en_titanet_large.onnx
printf 'VOICEPRINT_API_KEY=%s\nVOICEPRINT_MODEL=nemo_en_titanet_large\n' "$(openssl rand -hex 32)" > .env
docker compose up -d --build
curl http://localhost:8090/healthz    # {"ok":true,"model":"nemo_en_titanet_large","dim":192}

The model comes from sherpa-onnx's speaker-recognition models release (the tag really is spelled recongition). Use TitaNet-L: Lemur's built-in matching numbers are for it. The service keeps nothing on disk, and it runs with a read-only file system.

4. The summary model server

Pick one. Each serves the model under the name meeting-summary, with at least 64k tokens of context. The model names are placeholders: choose a 4-bit build of a 27B-class model that fits your card (see Choosing the summary model).

vLLM · NVIDIA cards, fastest 
docker run -d --name summary-llm --restart unless-stopped \
  --gpus all --ipc=host -p 8000:8000 \
  -v ~/.cache/huggingface:/root/.cache/huggingface \
  vllm/vllm-openai:<version> \
  --model <Hugging Face id of a 4-bit (AWQ or GPTQ) 27B-class Qwen model> \
  --served-model-name meeting-summary \
  --max-model-len 65536 \
  --gpu-memory-utilization 0.90 \
  --api-key "$LLM_API_KEY"
  • Pin the image to a version you've tested rather than latest.
  • If vLLM says the context doesn't fit, try --kv-cache-dtype fp8, or a smaller model. Don't go below the context your longest meetings need.
  • For a Qwen reasoning model, recent vLLM versions can return its thinking separately (--reasoning-parser qwen3). Lemur copes either way.
llama.cpp · any card, including AMD and unified memory 
docker run -d --name summary-llm --restart unless-stopped \
  --gpus all -p 8000:8000 -v /srv/models:/models \
  ghcr.io/ggml-org/llama.cpp:server-cuda \
  -m /models/<model>-Q4_K_M.gguf \
  --alias meeting-summary \
  -c 65536 -np 1 -ngl 99 --jinja \
  --host 0.0.0.0 --port 8000 \
  --api-key "$LLM_API_KEY"
  • -c 65536 is the context, and -np 1 gives all of it to one request (llama-server splits the context between parallel requests). -ngl 99 puts every layer on the card, and --jinja uses the model's own chat template.
  • For AMD cards, including the Ryzen AI Max in a Framework Desktop, use llama.cpp's Vulkan or ROCm image instead of server-cuda, and pass the card's devices (/dev/dri, plus /dev/kfd for ROCm) instead of --gpus all. Check llama.cpp's documentation for the current image names.
Ollama · simplest to try 
ollama pull <model>
cat > Modelfile <<'EOF'
FROM <model>
PARAMETER num_ctx 65536
EOF
ollama create meeting-summary -f Modelfile
Set num_ctx, or long meetings get cut

Ollama's default context is small. It doesn't give an error when a transcript is longer: it silently drops the start, and the summary misses most of the meeting. The Modelfile above fixes the context for every request.

  • Its OpenAI-compatible address is http://<server>:11434/v1.
  • Ollama has no keys. Let only LiteLLM reach it, for example by putting them on the same Docker network and not publishing port 11434.

5. LiteLLM

LiteLLM puts the summary model and the voiceprint service behind one address, and gives each person their own key that only works for those two models.

litellm/.env 
LITELLM_MASTER_KEY=sk-<random>      # your admin key; starts with sk-
LITELLM_SALT_KEY=sk-<random>        # encrypts what LiteLLM stores; never change it once set
POSTGRES_PASSWORD=<random>
LLM_API_KEY=<the key you gave the summary model server>
VOICEPRINT_API_KEY=<from the voiceprint service's .env>
litellm/compose.yaml 
services:
  litellm:
    image: ghcr.io/berriai/litellm:<version>   # pin a version you've tested
    restart: unless-stopped
    ports:
      - "4000:4000"
    command: ["--config", "/app/config.yaml", "--port", "4000"]
    volumes:
      - ./config.yaml:/app/config.yaml:ro
    environment:
      DATABASE_URL: postgresql://litellm:${POSTGRES_PASSWORD}@db:5432/litellm
      LITELLM_MASTER_KEY: ${LITELLM_MASTER_KEY}
      LITELLM_SALT_KEY: ${LITELLM_SALT_KEY}
      LLM_API_KEY: ${LLM_API_KEY}
      VOICEPRINT_API_KEY: ${VOICEPRINT_API_KEY}
    depends_on:
      - db
  db:
    image: postgres:16
    restart: unless-stopped
    environment:
      POSTGRES_DB: litellm
      POSTGRES_USER: litellm
      POSTGRES_PASSWORD: ${POSTGRES_PASSWORD}
    volumes:
      - pgdata:/var/lib/postgresql/data

volumes:
  pgdata:
litellm/config.yaml 
model_list:
  - model_name: meeting-summary              # the name Lemur asks for
    litellm_params:
      model: openai/meeting-summary          # "openai/" + the name your model server uses
      api_base: http://llm-host:8000/v1      # Ollama: http://ollama-host:11434/v1
      api_key: os.environ/LLM_API_KEY        # Ollama: any text
      timeout: 1200                          # long meetings can take minutes

  - model_name: speaker-embed-local          # Lemur's default voiceprint model name
    litellm_params:
      model: openai/nemo_en_titanet_large    # the voiceprint service ignores this name
      api_base: http://voiceprint-host:8090/v1
      api_key: os.environ/VOICEPRINT_API_KEY
    model_info:
      mode: embedding

general_settings:
  master_key: os.environ/LITELLM_MASTER_KEY
  database_url: os.environ/DATABASE_URL
  store_prompts_in_spend_logs: false         # don't copy transcripts into the database

# No fallbacks and no callbacks: nothing leaves your network.

Start it with docker compose up -d. Then make a key for each person, limited to the two models (shown here through the HTTPS name from step 6):

curl -s https://llm.example.lan/key/generate \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $LITELLM_MASTER_KEY" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"key_alias": "lemur-dana", "models": ["meeting-summary", "speaker-embed-local"]}'

The answer's "key" (sk-…) is Dana's summary key. Make one for Scriberr the same way, with only meeting-summary. A key can be blocked or deleted on its own later, without affecting anyone else.

6. HTTPS names

Put Scriberr and LiteLLM behind a reverse proxy with TLS, so audio and transcripts are encrypted on your network too. With Caddy:

Caddyfile 
scriberr.example.lan {
	tls internal
	reverse_proxy scriberr-host:8080
}

llm.example.lan {
	tls internal
	reverse_proxy litellm-host:4000
}

tls internal makes Caddy its own certificate authority. Each PC must trust it once: copy Caddy's root certificate (pki/authorities/local/root.crt in Caddy's data folder) to the PC and run:

certutil -user -addstore Root root.crt

Windows asks you to confirm. Instead, you can use certificates from your organisation's own authority, or a DNS-validated certificate for a domain you own. That works without opening anything to the internet.

nginx and Nginx Proxy Manager

Raise two defaults: client_max_body_size (an hour of meeting uploads about 115 MB; nginx allows 1 MB) and proxy_read_timeout (summaries can take several minutes; nginx waits 60 seconds). For example client_max_body_size 1g; and proxy_read_timeout 900s;.

7. Connecting the PCs

The easy way: set Lemur up on your own PC first. Then Settings → Account → Export setup file… makes a .lemursetup file holding the server addresses and model names, never keys. Send it to each person with their own keys. Their setup window imports it, and they paste their keys in.

By hand: in %APPDATA%\Lemur\settings.json (the manual covers the file):

"backend": {
  "type": "scriberr",
  "scriberr": { "baseUrl": "https://scriberr.example.lan", "apiKeySecret": "scriberr-api-key" }
},
"summary": {
  "baseUrl": "https://llm.example.lan/v1",   // LiteLLM; for the introductions check and meeting titles
  "model": "meeting-summary",
  "apiKeySecret": "summary-api-key",
  "maxTokens": 16384
},
"speakers": { "enabled": true, "model": "speaker-embed-local", "apiKeySecret": "voiceprint-api-key" }

Keys go into Windows Credential Manager, never the file. Copy each key, then pipe it in from the clipboard so it doesn't land in your terminal history:

Get-Clipboard | llmmr set-secret scriberr-api-key    # a Scriberr API key
Get-Clipboard | llmmr set-secret summary-api-key     # the person's LiteLLM key
llmmr settings                                       # "status: ready"

An empty speakers.baseUrl means "the same LiteLLM as the summary". The voiceprint key can be the same LiteLLM key: when voiceprint-api-key isn't stored and the address matches, the summary key is used.

8. Check it works

From a PC, in PowerShell (use curl.exe: plain curl is something else in Windows PowerShell):

$key = "sk-your-litellm-key"
curl.exe -s https://llm.example.lan/v1/models -H "Authorization: Bearer $key"

$body = '{"model":"meeting-summary","max_tokens":2000,"messages":[{"role":"user","content":"Reply with five words."}]}'
$body | curl.exe -s https://llm.example.lan/v1/chat/completions -H "Authorization: Bearer $key" -H "Content-Type: application/json" -d '@-'

curl.exe -s -o NUL -w "%{http_code}`n" https://scriberr.example.lan/api/v1/profiles/ -H "X-API-Key: your-scriberr-key"
  • The first lists meeting-summary and speaker-embed-local.
  • The second answers with a short reply. A thinking model may take a little while.
  • The third prints 200. It prints only the status on purpose: Scriberr's profile list includes the Hugging Face token, which shouldn't end up on your screen.

Then, in Lemur:

  1. Settings → Account → Test connection: every server passes.
  2. llmmr voices calibrate names the voiceprint model and says Lemur has matching numbers for it (only if voice recognition is on).
  3. Make a two-minute test call, or choose Start recording now while a video with people talking plays. Watch the steps in the Recordings window; the note should appear a minute or two after you stop.

Keeping it all on your network

Nothing in this chain calls a cloud service. To keep it that way:

  • The internet is only needed at setup: Docker images, the models from Hugging Face, and the voiceprint model from GitHub. After that you can block the servers' outgoing traffic. Scriberr may try to reach Hugging Face when it loads models; HF_HUB_OFFLINE=1 should stop that, but it's untested, so run a meeting through after you block it.
  • No cloud fallbacks. Don't add hosted models to LiteLLM's fallback lists. A fallback that quietly sends a transcript to a hosted API is the easiest way to lose "local".
  • No logging callbacks (Langfuse, Helicone…) in LiteLLM, and keep store_prompts_in_spend_logs: false.
  • Away from the office or home, connect over a VPN (WireGuard, Tailscale, Headscale, NetBird) rather than opening ports.
  • TLS even on your own network. Audio and transcripts are the sensitive part, and they travel with every meeting.

Known limits

  • Scriberr waits 5 minutes for a summary. A slow model can't summarize long meetings (see above).
  • Scriberr keeps every job's audio. Clean it up by hand for now.
  • Scriberr has one library. Everyone's meetings sit in the same Scriberr account, visible to whoever can sign in to it. That's fine for one person or a household. For a team, give each person their own Scriberr, or wait for the planned multi-user transcription path.
  • The OpenAI-compatible transcription option (backend.type: "openai") needs a server that returns speaker-separated diarized_json. Without it the transcript has no speakers. No local server with that output has been tried; Scriberr is the tested path. If the server also sends word times (a words list), Lemur uses them to split a line between you and someone who cut in.
  • One meeting at a time per PC. Several people sharing one server queue on its card. Scriberr ran two jobs at once on one RTX 3090 measured; the model server's own batching decides the rest.
